# Iowa From Above: 2:40 video script

Use four members total. Each of the other three members speaks for 30 seconds. You have 30 seconds for the opening, 30 seconds for the website demonstration, and 10 seconds for the ending. Rehearse aloud with a stopwatch; all titles and the final card count toward the total.

The opening shows a real consequence of a water-quality problem. The demonstration then explains how the app helps someone understand the geography, check the evidence and follow up on a place. Keep the delivery conversational. Pause on information the viewer needs to read.

Show all four full names and the app title during the opening. Replace [Name] with the correct speaker's name. The opening includes a single sentence that states the purpose and audience.

## 0:00 to 0:30: You

> In June 2026, central Iowa restricted lawn watering because high nitrate levels were limiting drinking water treatment. We wanted to make the connection between what happens upstream and the people affected downstream easier to understand. I'm [Name]. Iowa From Above helps students and residents understand Iowa's environmental problems and find ways to help through a 3D website and an iPhone app.

Start with the river in the actual app. Add the short factual caption 'Central Iowa, June 2026' and a readable CIWW source credit. Show the app title and all four full names. After briefly introducing the speaker, show the landscape while explaining the upstream and downstream connection.

## 0:30 to 1:00: You

> This is High Trestle Bridge over the Des Moines River, one of twelve places in the journey. Flying closer opens a reading automatically. It explains how nutrients from fertilizer and manure can reach waterways through runoff and drainage, and why that matters downstream. You can look at the surrounding land as you explore responses like wetlands and stream buffers. Other stops cover flooding, erosion, and habitat loss.

Approach the bridge with the cursor away from its label. Let the reading open automatically. Show People and Response. Keep the river visible beside the card. Briefly show a wetland or flood lesson to establish that the journey covers other issues too.

## 1:00 to 1:30: Member 2

> I'm [Name]. Each lesson connects to information you can check. USGS stations show water measurements with dates and units. Watershed boundaries explain where water drains, and FEMA layers show mapped flood hazards. You can ask Avery, our AI guide, what the information means for the place you're exploring. We also explain its limits: a river measurement doesn't tell you whether treated tap water is safe.

Show an actual station and hold on its observation date, units and source. Switch briefly to watershed and FEMA layers, with mapped-hazard wording visible. Ask Avery 'How can farming affect this watershed?' and show the actual response.

## 1:30 to 2:00: Member 3

> I'm [Name]. We connected the website to an iPhone app so you can revisit these places. Choose Send to phone and scan the QR code to open the same location. Apple Maps provides directions, and you can read conservation ideas and save notes or photos. A student visiting a wetland, for example, can connect the lesson to what they notice in person.

Show the real QR handoff and make the identical place name visible on both screens. Enlarge the phone recording to show What helps, Apple Maps directions, and Journal. The student wetland visit is an example of a possible use, not a claim that a field trial has already happened.

## 2:00 to 2:30: Member 4

> I'm [Name]. We used TypeScript, Next.js, CesiumJS and Google 3D Tiles for the website, and Swift with SwiftUI for iPhone. Both apps use shared place IDs, so a checked QR link opens the matching location. We also save flight position separately from lesson progress. That lets us restore the location while preserving which lessons a user has completed.

Show knownPlaceId in app/companionLinks.ts for the ID check, then a short actual reload/resume recording. If you show save code, use the real FlightSession fields in app/flightSession.ts. Put MapKit, Xcode and Groq API in the tools caption. Keep code and source credits readable, and keep API keys out of the recording.

## 2:30 to 2:40: You

> We want people to recognize these problems in places they know, and leave knowing what they can do next.

Return to the river and its place card. Show the app logo and let the ending finish naturally. Keep the end card inside the 2:40 total.

## Recording and editing

Use the app's dark gray colors and the website font. Keep overlays short. A label such as "Water quality" or "Same place on your phone" is enough when the screen recording explains the rest.

Show each teammate for two or three seconds, then keep their voice playing over the demonstration. Use one clear website-to-phone transition at the QR handoff. Give data and code enough time on screen to read. Keep a phone mockup still while someone needs to read it.

Keep all four voices at a similar volume, with quiet music underneath. Add readable subtitles with no more than two lines at once. Export a clear 1920x1080 video. Rehearse against the actual footage so the words match the action.

Record real successful interactions. Leave observation dates and map credits visible. Use a tested signed iPhone build for a camera demonstration, or show the working QR-image import. Label a simulator capture if you use one. Do not present the app as published in the App Store before publication.

For the opening fact, use the CIWW source below and the date June 9, 2026. It concerns source-water quality and treatment capacity. CIWW also stated that delivered drinking water continued meeting standards.

## Submission

The official rules require participant names, the app name, one clear purpose sentence, the audience, tools and coding languages, and a demonstration of functionality. The complete video must run between one and three minutes and be Public on YouTube or Vimeo.

The 2026 rules require accurate disclosure of AI assistance and significant student contributions and technical understanding. Describe the help used and each student's real work. Check the participating district's current judging instructions; the supplied rubric is marked 2021.
